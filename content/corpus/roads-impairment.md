Alcohol's earliest effects land on exactly the capacities riding spends fastest: hazard perception, scanning discipline, balance, reaction time — and self-assessment, the ability to notice that the other capacities are degraded. That last one is the trap. The impaired rider is confident precisely because the instrument that would measure the impairment is the impaired instrument. This is why "one beer, an hour ago, easy trail" is not a calculation to win but a calculation to refuse: any trailside impairment math is being performed by the taxed brain the math is about.

Legal thresholds do not rescue the calculation. Vehicle limits are prosecution lines, not safety lines, and an active-riding machine on uneven terrain demands more continuous judgment and balance than driving a car, not the same. Nor does the absence of traffic enforcement on trails change anything — trees, slopes, and water crossings do not check papers, and the physics of a missed hazard is identical with no officer for fifty miles.

The pre-commitment structure the course teaches everywhere applies here in its cleanest form: riding days are dry days, decided before the cooler is packed, so no in-the-moment arithmetic is ever required. The same reasoning extends past alcohol to anything that taxes the same systems — impairing medications, other substances, and serious fatigue, which degrades scanning and judgment in ways riders reliably underestimate.
