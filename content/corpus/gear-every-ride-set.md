Five items ride every time, defined by the fact of riding rather than the length or difficulty of the trip: a fitted, rated helmet with the strap fastened; eye protection; over-the-ankle boots; gloves; and long sleeves with long pants. The set is small on purpose — a short list actually gets worn.

Each item answers an exposure that does not scale down with easy rides. Eye protection is the clearest case: a branch at face height, a stone thrown by a companion's tire, or an insect at speed does not slow down because you did, and eyes have no cheap repair. Over-the-ankle boots exist because feet and ankles live next to pegs, rocks, and — in a tip-over — the machine itself; ankle support and a stiff sole turn several categories of injury into bruises. Gloves keep grip when hands are sweaty, cold, or scraped, and hands are what humans land on. Long sleeves and pants are abrasion coverage: trail surfaces are graters, and skin is the one item of gear you cannot replace at the store.

The organizing principle is dress for the crash, not for the ride: choose protection for the worst five seconds of the day, then solve comfort inside that constraint with layers and ventilation — never by deleting an item. The five-item set is the floor. Conditional gear (chest protection, knee guards, rain layers, cold-weather kit) stacks on top as the ride demands.
