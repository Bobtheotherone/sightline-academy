A ride plan is only useful if it lives with someone who is not on the ride. Its job is to work when you cannot: if the check-in never comes, the person holding the plan knows when to act and where to point help. That gives it three required parts. Where — the specific riding area and intended route, specific enough to narrow a search from a county to a drainage. When — a check-in time, which is the trigger that makes the whole arrangement mechanical instead of dependent on someone eventually feeling worried. What then — the agreed action if the check-in is missed, decided in advance so the holder is not improvising during the worst hour to improvise.

A plan written down and left in your own vehicle at the trailhead rescues no one; the person who needs it is by definition somewhere else. And the plan pairs with, but does not replace, communication you carry. The two lines back each other up because they fail differently: a phone can die, break, or sit outside coverage — all most likely on exactly the remote, cold, wet rides where help matters most — while the ride plan is passive and keeps working through every one of those failures.

Closing the loop is part of the system: check in when you are out, every time, so that the one missed check-in that matters is believed.
