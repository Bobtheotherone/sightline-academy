ATV tires run at pressures that would look like a mistake on a car — often in the single digits of PSI. At those numbers, small absolute changes are huge relative changes: losing two or three PSI from a five-PSI tire is roughly half the tire's pressure gone, and the machine will handle differently even though the tire still looks round from above. That is why the inspection rule is a gauge, not a thumb-press or an eyeball.

The low pressure is not a compromise; it is the design. A soft, conforming carcass lets the tire drape itself over roots and rocks, putting rubber in contact where a stiff tire would bridge and skip. The knobs get the credit, but the conforming footprint is half the grip story. It only works inside a narrow design range: too low and the tire squirms, overheats, and can unseat from the rim under side load; too high and the footprint shrinks, the ride harshens, and the conforming behavior disappears.

Mismatched pressures side to side are their own hazard — the machine pulls, and it pulls hardest during braking, exactly when you want it tracking straight. The habit that catches all of this costs about a minute: gauge all four before the ride, match them to the figure on the machine's label, and treat any tire that lost pressure overnight as a question that needs an answer, not a top-up.
