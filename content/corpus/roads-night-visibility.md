Darkness attacks the same resource that speed rules protect: sight lines. The honest speed limit — never faster than you can stop within the distance you can see — does not change at night; the visible distance does, collapsing to the reach of a headlight. Everything terrain reading depends on gets harder in the beam's flat light: depth flattens, ruts and holes lose their shadows, wet and ice read as dry, and the far–near–sides scan runs on a fraction of its daytime information. Dusk is the sneaky version: eyes adapt gradually, so riders keep daytime speeds well past the point their actual sight lines justify them.

Night also inverts the visibility relationship with everyone else. Being seen becomes as hard as seeing: working lights front and rear stop being an inspection checkbox and become the whole difference between existing and not existing to other trail users and to any traffic near crossings. This is why the walkaround checks lights even for rides planned entirely in daylight — plans run long, and the ride that ends at dusk was usually planned to end at four.

The planning posture: treat riding into darkness as a decision made deliberately in advance — with lights verified, speeds pre-lowered, and familiar terrain chosen — not a condition that arrives mid-ride and gets absorbed. And where night riding is restricted by local rules, that restriction is one more case of regulation encoding physics.
