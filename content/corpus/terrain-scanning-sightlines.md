Vision drives steering more directly than most riders realize: the machine goes where the eyes lock. That is the mechanism behind target fixation — a rider frightened by a rock stares at the rock, and the stare quietly steers toward it. The countermeasure is a deliberate scan pattern: far, near, sides, repeat. Far, to spot problems while there is still time and distance to respond; near, to place the wheels around what is arriving now; sides, to keep track of escape routes and whatever is about to enter the trail. The rotation rations attention so no single hazard owns the eyes, and when avoidance is needed, the trained move is to look at the gap, not the obstacle.

Sight lines set the honest speed limit regardless of what any sign or comfort level says: never ride faster than you can stop within the distance you can currently see. The trail's posted difficulty does not change over a blind crest, but your available information drops to zero, so speed must drop with it. Dust from a rider ahead, low sun, rain, and dusk all shrink sight lines the same way — and each deserves the same response as a blind corner.

Scanning is also the delivery system for everything else in terrain reading: slopes, surfaces, and water can only be assessed if the eyes arrive on them early enough for the assessment to matter.
