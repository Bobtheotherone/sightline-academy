Most ATVs are single-rider machines, and the reason lives in the stability model. A rider-active machine budgets its balance around one operator who continuously shifts weight — forward on climbs, back on descents, uphill on traverses. A passenger breaks that budget three ways at once, even a passenger who does everything right. Their mass raises the combined center of gravity and shifts it rearward and up, shrinking the stability margin on every slope. Their weight moves on its own schedule — bracing, sliding, reacting late — adding an input the operator can neither predict nor control. And their body occupies the space the operator needs to move through, pinning the operator in place on exactly the terrain where weight shifting matters most.

None of this is about the passenger's behavior or the ride's speed. The center-of-gravity change and the blocked operator exist at walking pace, and low-speed maneuvers on side slopes are where rider-active weight shifts do their most important work. The seat length is not an invitation; it is room for the operator to move.

Machines designed for two exist — true tandem ATVs and many side-by-sides — with longer wheelbases, dedicated seating, and stability budgets that include the second person. The rule that follows is simple: the machine's design and label decide the occupancy, and a single-rider machine carries one.
