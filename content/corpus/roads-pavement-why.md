The rule against riding ATVs on paved roads is physics first and law second. The same design choices that make an ATV brilliant off-road invert into liabilities on asphalt. Low-pressure knobby tires are built to conform to soft, uneven ground; on high-grip pavement the tall knobs and soft carcass squirm under the machine, making steering vague and response unpredictable, especially in the small, quick corrections that road riding constantly demands. Many ATVs also use a solid rear axle — both rear wheels locked together — which soft surfaces forgive by letting a wheel slip slightly in turns; grippy pavement forgives nothing, so the machine fights the rider through corners and pushes wide.

The result is a machine that handles worst exactly where the traffic is fastest and the margins are thinnest, surrounded by drivers who are not looking for it. On top of the handling problem sits the visibility problem: ATVs are lower, narrower, and rarer than what drivers scan for, and driver expectation is a real component of road safety — people reliably fail to see vehicle shapes they are not looking for.

This is why the near-universal pattern in regulation is that public paved roads are off-limits or tightly restricted, with narrow exceptions like designated crossings. The law follows the machine's design; understanding the design is what makes the rule stick without needing enforcement nearby.
