Every kilogram on the racks re-tunes the machine. Cargo raises the combined center of gravity and moves it toward whichever rack carries it, which shrinks stability margins on slopes, lengthens braking, and lightens the steering end if the load rides on the rear. The machine's label lists rack capacities and total capacity for the same reason a helmet carries a rating: the numbers encode the design's assumptions, and exceeding them means operating a machine no one engineered.

The loading practice that keeps margins: heavy and low beats light and high; balanced side to side; secured so it cannot shift — a loose load is a passenger with no hands, moving the center of gravity on its own schedule mid-maneuver; and nothing blocking lights, the rider's movement, or the rider's line of sight. After loading, the first minutes of the ride are a re-calibration, not a resumption: braking distances, turn-in, and slope behavior have all changed, and the rider's habits were trained on the unloaded machine.

Towing multiplies the same story: a trailer adds mass that pushes on descents, cuts corners on turns, and can jackknife under hard braking, so tow-rated hitch points, modest speeds, wider lines, and earlier braking are the pattern. And the terrain calculus changes with the load — the side slope that was fine unloaded may not be fine today, which is a today-assessment, not a memory.
