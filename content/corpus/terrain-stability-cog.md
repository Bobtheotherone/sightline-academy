An ATV stays upright as long as the combined center of gravity — machine plus rider plus cargo — stays over the polygon formed by the tire contact patches. Every stability conversation is a conversation about where that point sits and what is moving it. Slope tilts the polygon so the same center of gravity sits closer to the downhill edge. Cargo raises the center of gravity and, if mounted high or loose, moves it unpredictably. Rider position is the one input under continuous control: shifting weight uphill, forward on climbs, or rearward on descents moves the combined point back toward the middle of the polygon.

The machine reports its own margins if you listen. Wheels going light on the uphill side of a traverse are the direct signal that the center of gravity is approaching the support edge — that is information to act on by changing the situation (slow, adjust line, put weight uphill, or stop and rethink), never a limit to probe further. Width and a low stance buy margin, but no ATV is immune to the geometry; side slopes cause tip-overs precisely because they feel manageable right up until the wheels lighten.

The awareness-level takeaway: read every slope, load, and line as a question about where the combined center of gravity is headed, and keep a habit of noticing what is currently spending your margin.
