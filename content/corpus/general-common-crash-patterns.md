Serious ATV crashes look varied on the surface and monotonous underneath. Across years of incident analysis, the same short list of rider decisions keeps appearing, alone or stacked: no helmet, which converts survivable head impacts into fatal ones and appears in a majority of ATV fatalities; riding on paved roads, where the machine's off-road design turns against it amid the fastest traffic; carrying passengers on single-rider machines, spending stability margin the design never had; impairment, which taxes judgment and self-assessment first; riders on machines they don't fit — most often children on adult-size ATVs, one of the most consistent threads in the fatality data; and speed beyond sight lines, which removes the time that every other skill needs in order to matter.

The monotony is the good news. A hazard list this short and this behavioral is a hazard list a rider controls almost entirely: none of the six requires talent, equipment money, or luck to eliminate — each is a decision available before the ride begins. This is the empirical backbone under the whole course's design: the walkaround, the gear rules, the fit checks, the road module, and the pre-commitment habit each exist to take one or more items on this list off the table permanently, rather than asking the rider to out-skill them in the moment.
