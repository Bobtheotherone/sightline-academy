Hills concentrate three questions into one piece of ground: can you see the top, can you stop partway, and what happens if the answer changes halfway up? A climb with a blind crest is a commitment to whatever is on the other side — another rider coming down, a washout, a sudden drop — which is why crests get treated like blind corners: assume occupancy, keep speed inside your sight line, and favor lines with visibility. Descents move the weight question forward: gravity is already loading the front, so the margin conversation is about staying back and controlling speed early, because speed gained on a descent compounds while the room to shed it shrinks.

Side slopes deserve their own respect. Traversing across a slope puts the tilt directly into the stability geometry, and the failure mode — wheels lightening on the uphill side — arrives quietly. The awareness-level rules: prefer lines that climb or descend straight rather than cut across steep faces, treat any slope that makes you want to lean hard uphill as a slope to reconsider, and remember that surface matters as much as angle — a moderate slope of wet clay or loose gravel behaves like a steep one.

The overarching habit is assessment before commitment. Stop where stopping is cheap, read the whole feature, and pick the moment you would abandon the line before you take it. Hills punish improvisation more than any other common terrain.
