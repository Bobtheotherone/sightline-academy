A large share of serious off-road incidents happen on terrain the rider knows well, to riders who are past the beginner stage. The pattern has a mechanism: repeated exposure without a bad outcome quietly lowers *perceived* risk, while the actual risk of the place stays where it always was. The trail did not get safer on your twelfth ride; your attention got cheaper.

This matters because trails are dynamic and memory is static. The line you remember is the line from last time — before the washout, before the storm dropped a limb across the blind crest, before another rider dug a rut through the crossing. Riding from memory means riding the trail that used to be there.

The countermeasure is not fear; it's a deliberate re-arming of attention. Give the dozen-th ride first-ride eyes: scan as if the crest hides something, because sometimes it does. Some riders use a simple trigger — any time they notice the thought "I know this part," they treat it as a cue to look harder rather than relax. Confidence and complacency feel identical from the inside; the difference is that confidence says *I know this trail's patterns* while complacency says *this trail can't surprise me anymore*. Only one of those sentences is ever true.
