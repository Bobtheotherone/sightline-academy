Water hides everything that matters about itself: depth, bottom composition, holes, drop-offs, submerged obstacles, and the true push of the current. That is why the first rule of crossings is absolute — water you cannot read is water you do not ride. Opaque water, water too deep to judge from the bank, or a crossing whose far side you cannot see is a walk-away, not a puzzle to solve from the saddle.

For water you can read, the assessment happens from the bank, on foot if needed and safe to do so. Look for the established crossing point — traffic concentrates where crossing works. Judge depth against a fixed reference you can actually see, and remember that moving water is stronger than it looks: the force on the machine and on a wading rider grows quickly with both depth and speed, and knee-deep current has taken vehicles far heavier than an ATV. Check the exits: a crossable channel with a steep, greasy, or undercut far bank is not a crossing, it is a trap with an entrance.

Even good crossings change. The creek that was ankle-deep last month reads differently after two days of rain, and upstream weather you never saw can move a crossing from fine to dangerous between morning and afternoon. Every crossing gets today's assessment, and the option to turn around is part of every crossing plan.
