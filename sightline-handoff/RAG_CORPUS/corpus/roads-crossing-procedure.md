Where a trail legitimately meets a paved road at a designated or unavoidable crossing point, the procedure is exposure math: every element exists to minimize the seconds spent on the hostile surface and the angles from which traffic can arrive unseen. Stop fully before the pavement — a complete stop, not a rolling glance, because judging closing car speeds takes stationary seconds and cars arrive faster than trail-tuned perception expects. Look both ways, then look again; the second look catches the vehicle that the first look's timing missed. Cross straight across, at a right angle to the road: the perpendicular line is the shortest path, presents your profile to traffic for the least time, and keeps the machine's road time to a few seconds. Do not linger, turn along the road, or ride the shoulder to a better entry — each of those converts a crossing into road riding, with all the handling and visibility problems that entails.

Group crossings add one rule: cross one at a time, each rider making their own stop and their own traffic assessment. A follower who inherits the leader's gap inherits a gap measured for someone else, several seconds stale. And the procedure has an environmental floor: if sight lines along the road are short — a hill, a curve, dust, dusk — the crossing waits or moves. A crossing you cannot fully see is water you cannot read.
