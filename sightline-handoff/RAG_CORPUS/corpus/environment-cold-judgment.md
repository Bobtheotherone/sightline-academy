Environmental stress taxes judgment before it taxes anything visible. Cold's earliest costs are cognitive — slower thinking, worse risk assessment, and an increasingly seductive preference for pressing on because stopping feels like effort. Wet accelerates all of it, because water strips body heat far faster than air, and a machine's self-made wind turns mild air into real chill at trail speeds. By the time hands fumble a brake lever, the rider's decision-making has been degraded for a while.

This ordering has a practical consequence: make the important decisions early, while you are still the sharp version of yourself. A weather turn ninety minutes into a remote loop is the moment to decide — right then, warm and thinking clearly — exactly what conditions trigger turning back, rather than deferring to a later self who will be colder, wetter, and worse at exactly this kind of math. Racing the weather to finish a loop is the classic compounding error: it spends reaction margin at the moment the trail is getting slicker and sight lines shorter.

The preparation side is unglamorous and effective: check the forecast for the whole ride window and the terrain you will actually be in, pack the wind-blocking and insulating layers for the far end of the ride rather than the trailhead, and treat "it turned earlier than forecast" as a normal event with a pre-decided response, not a surprise.
