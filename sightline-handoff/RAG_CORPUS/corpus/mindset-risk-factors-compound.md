Risk factors on a ride don't add — they multiply. Each factor draws down a different safety margin, and the margins back each other up, so removing several at once is worse than the arithmetic suggests. Unfamiliar terrain raises the odds of a surprise. Fading light shortens the distance at which you can see the surprise coming. Riding alone removes the person who would have helped after the surprise arrived. Any one of those is manageable; together they turn a small mistake into a serious event, because the factor that would normally catch the failure is the one you also removed.

The same multiplication shows up in the classic pairings from crash analysis: impairment raises the chance of a crash while a missing helmet raises the cost of that same crash. Speed beyond your sight line raises both at once — more likely to meet something, less time to do anything about it.

The practical habit is to count factors before and during a ride, not to grade each one in isolation. A ride that is dark *or* unfamiliar *or* solo can be a fine ride. A ride that is quietly becoming dark *and* unfamiliar *and* solo deserves a deliberate decision, made out loud, rather than a drift. Experienced riders describe this as protecting margin: you can spend one margin on purpose, but spending three at once means nothing is left to absorb the mistake you didn't plan to make.
