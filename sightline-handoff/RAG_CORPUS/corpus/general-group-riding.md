Groups make rides safer in the big picture — help is present, breakdowns are inconveniences — while adding failure modes of their own, and group-riding conventions exist to close those specific gaps. Spacing answers the dust and reaction problem: following too close means riding inside the leader's dust plume with sight lines near zero, so the working rule is far enough back to see and stop, stretching further as dust worsens. The pre-ride briefing answers the assumption problem: route, pace, planned stops, and turnaround criteria said out loud, so no one is improvising navigation or silently enduring a pace beyond their comfort — and pace is set by the least experienced rider, not the most eager one.

Lead and sweep structure answers the lost-rider problem: an experienced rider navigates in front, another experienced rider runs last and never passes anyone, so nobody can silently disappear off the back with a breakdown. The junction rule closes the same gap from another side: each rider waits at any turn until the rider behind them is visible and sees the turn taken — a chain that keeps the group connected without requiring anyone to match anyone else's speed between junctions.

Hand signals or agreed stops handle communication where engines eat voices. And group pressure — the quiet pull to keep up, skip the walkaround, or push past a turnaround time — is itself a named hazard, which pre-ride agreements exist specifically to disarm.
