Where ATVs and road traffic legitimately meet — designated crossings, mixed-use rural routes where local law allows, trailheads on road shoulders — the safety problem is shared, and each side is missing something the other has. Drivers are missing expectation: people reliably fail to perceive vehicle shapes they are not scanning for, and an ATV is lower, narrower, slower, and rarer than the cars and trucks a driver's search pattern is tuned to. A driver can look directly at an unexpected vehicle and not register it — the looked-but-failed-to-see pattern documented across motorcycle and bicycle traffic research applies with full force to ATVs.

Riders are missing protection and speed compatibility, which means the rider's job is to assume invisibility and behave in ways that repair the driver's expectation gap: crossing at designated points where drivers have some reason to anticipate machines, making full stops that create clean decision moments instead of ambiguous rolling approaches, using lights even in daylight, wearing high-visibility elements, and never assuming eye contact means having been seen.

Drivers near trail country carry the mirror obligations: expect machines at marked crossings, read dust plumes near road edges as information, and give crossing riders the same clean margin given to any slow vehicle. The mutual principle is the one that governs all mixed traffic: predictability is a gift each user gives the other, and surprises are the shared enemy.
